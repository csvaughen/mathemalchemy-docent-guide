# Tess the Tortoise

## What it is
Tess is the exhibit's beloved mascot — a knitted tortoise with a ceramic shell — journeying along **Zeno's Path**, a quilted trail whose paver segments shrink as she approaches her destination, embodying Zeno's paradox of motion. The **Riemann Wall** runs alongside the path, carrying a **Hilbert's Hotel** billboard (the paradox of the infinite hotel that's always "full" yet always has room for one more guest). The path leads to **Integral Hill**, which stages two rival ways of measuring the same shape side by side: **Lebesgue Terraces** — wooden platforms shaped as genuine level curves of a two-variable function, so climbing them is literally slicing the hill horizontally — and, visible from the top, the **Riemann Cliffs** — 300-plus hexagonal dowels cut to varied heights forming a "noisy" height graph, the vertical-rectangle approach from Calc I. (Distinct from the unrelated café scene also called a "terrace" — see [[The Terrace]].)

**Zeno's Path is a geometric series you can walk.** The path is tiled in sections, each half as long as the one before, running all the way to Integral Hill. Measured as fractions of the whole path, the sections go ½, ¼, ⅛, … and add up to exactly the whole: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + ⋯ = 1. After *n* sections Tess has covered 1 − 1/2ⁿ of the way. The part still ahead is always exactly as long as the section she just finished, so it never shrinks to nothing, and yet every point short of the hill is eventually passed. Infinitely many sections, one finite path: that is what it means for an infinite sum to [[Infinite Series and Convergence|converge]], and it is calculus's answer to Zeno's dichotomy paradox (the argument that she must first go halfway, then half of what remains, forever). Only so many sections can actually be built, so the path shows the beginning of the sum, not all of it. The classroom version, alongside the Ball Arches' series that never stops → [[Docent Program/Curriculum/Calculus|Calculus]], and as a worksheet that starts on this path and ends inside the arches → [[Infinite Length Finite Volume|Infinite Length, Finite Volume]].

**Her shell is a heptagonal tiling of the hyperbolic plane.** Liz Paley threw a flat clay disk and, once it stiffened, impressed the tiling into it with a serrated tracing wheel before nudging the disk into a shell shape — so the pattern is the flat *disk* picture of hyperbolic space, curved into three dimensions. This is the striking part: seven-sided tiles **cannot** fit together edge to edge on a flat surface, because a regular heptagon's corners are a shade too wide to close up around a point. Give the surface some negative curvature and they close perfectly → [[Hyperbolic Geometry]].

**Tess pulls a Sierpiński tetrahedral kite** on a string behind her — a tetrahedron built of four half-size tetrahedra, each of those built of four more, holes within holes. Its brass-rod frame was soldered by Bronna Butler. The shape is the best possible kite and the reason is arithmetic: halving the edge length quarters a face's area, and there are four copies, so **the total surface area never changes from one stage to the next** — while halving the edge cuts each piece's volume to an eighth, and four eighths is a half, so **the volume falls to zero.** A kite wants exactly that, all surface and no weight. It also lands the object on a strange number: four copies at half scale gives dimension log 4 / log 2 = **2 exactly**, so a thing you can hold in three dimensions is, by the measure fractals use, a two-dimensional object → [[Fractals and Self-Similarity]].

## Key vocabulary
- **Zeno's Paradox** — motion seems impossible because reaching a goal requires infinitely many halfway steps.
- **Level curve** — the set of points where a function of two variables takes the same value; each Lebesgue Terrace is one.
- **Lebesgue integral** — measuring by horizontal slices (function value), vs. the Riemann integral's vertical slices (domain) — staged here as terraces vs. cliffs.
- **Hilbert's Hotel** — a hotel with infinitely many rooms, always full, that can still make room for infinitely many new guests — a hands-on paradox of countable infinity.
