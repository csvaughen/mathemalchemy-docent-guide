# Mathematical History and the Long Conversation

**In the exhibit:** [[Stack of Books]] (the stack, and the portraits bookmarked inside it) · [[The Great Doodle Page]] (where the conversation is still going)

Mathematics is the only field where a 2,300-year-old textbook is still *correct*. Aristotle's physics is read as history; Euclid's *[Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements)* is read as mathematics. Its proofs hold today for exactly the reasons they held in Alexandria, and any visitor can check one on the back of an envelope. Almost nothing else humans have written survives that way.

So the [[Stack of Books]] isn't a display of old objects. It's a timeline of one argument that has run for three thousand years and has never once had to be restarted — Vedic altar-builders, the Chinese *Nine Chapters*, Euclid, al-Khwārizmī, on up through a *Scientific American* column and a book about weaving. Everyone in the stack was reading someone earlier, and someone later was reading them.

## Eternal theorems, chosen axioms

The stack states the idea and then complicates it, in the same volume.

Euclid's *Elements* is bookmarked with portraits of **[Nikolai Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Lobachevsky)** and **[János Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A1nos_Bolyai)** — the two mathematicians who, twenty-one centuries later, finally settled what generations had been trying to do. They showed that Euclid's fifth postulate (roughly: through a point off a line there's exactly one parallel) *cannot* be proved from his other four. Drop it, assume something else, and you don't get a contradiction — you get a different geometry, every bit as consistent as Euclid's.

That's the honest version of "eternal truth," and it's worth two minutes with a visitor:

- An **axiom** is a starting truth you *accept* without proof. It is a choice.
- A **theorem** is what follows from those axioms. Once proved, it never expires.

Euclid's theorems are permanent. Euclid's *starting assumptions* turned out to be optional. Both of those facts are true, and they are sitting in the same book on the same table. The geometry Bolyai and Lobachevsky opened up is crocheted into [[The Garden and Reef]] — see [[Hyperbolic Geometry]].

## The bookmarks are the conversation

Each book in the stack has a portrait tucked inside it, and the portrait is nearly always someone from a later century who was still working on what that book started:

- **Euclid → Lobachevsky and Bolyai.** Two thousand years to answer one question about parallel lines.
- **[H.S.M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) → [M.C. Escher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._C._Escher).** Not a metaphor: they wrote to each other for years, and Escher's hyperbolic tessellations came out of it. An artist and a geometer teaching each other.
- **[Martin Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Gardner) → [Doris Schattschneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doris_Schattschneider) → [Marjorie Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Rice).** Gardner wrote a magazine column; a San Diego homemaker with no training past high school read it and found new pentagon tilings nobody knew existed; Schattschneider, a professional mathematician, verified and published them. Rice's tiling is the border of [[The Great Doodle Page]].
- **Penner & Harer → [Maryam Mirzakhani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryam_Mirzakhani).** Her favorite book, bookmarked with her face — and her handwritten notes appear again, elsewhere in the installation, on [[The Great Doodle Page]].
- **[Magnus Wenninger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Wenninger) → [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horton_Conway).** A Benedictine monk's polyhedron-building manual, marked with Conway — and with the xkcd strip that memorialized him.

The point isn't that mathematics has a history. Every field has a history. The point is that mathematics is the field where the *earlier side of the conversation is still speaking* — where reading Euclid is not scholarship about the past but a live technical exchange with someone who died before the Roman Empire.

## Not a museum of dead men

The stack quietly refuses the standard cast list. Alongside Euclid and Weyl sit **[Mary Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Somerville)**, whose "translation" of Laplace added so much explanation it became the Cambridge textbook; a bookmark of **[Katherine Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Johnson)**; Mirzakhani; Schattschneider and Rice. Two of the books aren't mathematics at all — a Bauhaus book on weaving and an account of Polynesian navigation — because the conversation has always included people who were doing mathematics without calling it that. See [[Women in Mathematics]].
