# Activity: Infinite Length, Finite Volume

A scaffolded worksheet for a **Calculus 2** series unit, built around two objects in
[Mathemalchemy](https://mathemalchemy.org): [[Tess the Tortoise]]'s tiled path and the two
[[Ball Arches]]. It ends on the result the arches were built to show — one arch is
infinitely long, and the spheres making it up hold a finite total volume.

This one runs **in a classroom**, not in the gallery. Nothing in it asks students to look
at, measure or count anything in the room with the exhibit; every length is given as a
multiple of the first ball's diameter, so the mathematics works from the page alone.

## Materials
- **[[Infinite Length Finite Volume - Worksheet.pdf|The worksheet (PDF, 9 pages)]]** —
  six parts with room to work. Students need a calculator and nothing else.

An answer key with teaching notes is available from the docent coordinator.

## How it runs
The six parts build in order and split cleanly if a class period runs out.

1. **Zeno's Path** — the geometric series ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + ⋯ = 1, derived rather than
   recalled, on a path a tortoise actually walks → [[Zeno's Paradoxes and Limits]].
2. **The short arch** — a geometric series whose ratio is $(4/5)^{2/3}$, so students have
   to prove it is geometric before they can sum it. Ends with the fraction of the arch
   still to come after ball 60.
3. **The long arch** — the same picture, a different formula, and the opposite answer.
   The $n$th-term test says nothing, the ratio test is inconclusive, and a $p$-series
   comparison settles it.
4. **The volumes** — students commit to a prediction, then cube the diameter law and
   watch $p = \tfrac23$ become $p = 2$. The Basel sum $\pi^2/6$ turns up three terms in.
5. **Why two-thirds** — generalising the exponent to $p$, and finding the values of $p$
   for which the diameters diverge while the volumes converge.
6. **The Koch snowflake** — infinite perimeter around finite area, the same trick in a
   different costume, elsewhere in the same installation → [[Fractals and Self-Similarity]].

**Parts 1 through 4 are the core** and fill roughly a 50-minute period; all six run 75 to
90 minutes, or two sittings. Part 5 is the best mathematics on the sheet and the part most
worth keeping if there is time for one more.

## What students need first
Geometric series and the $p$-series test. The ratio test, the $n$th-term test and the
integral test all appear, but each is used rather than taught, and the worksheet says
what it is doing at every step. Question 3.9 uses an integral estimate and can be skipped
without breaking anything after it.

## Where it fits
The full teacher-facing picture of the series unit in the exhibit is on the
[[Docent Program/Curriculum/Calculus|Calculus]] page. The worked derivation the
worksheet is built on — both diameter formulas, both length series and both volume sums,
done exactly — is
**[[Ball Arches and Infinite Series.pdf|Ball Arches and Infinite Series (PDF)]]**.

The worksheet quotes no ball count for the short arch, because no published source gives
one. Its volume figures are sums over infinitely many idealized spheres whose diameters
decrease according to the two formulas, and make no claim about the material the spheres
are made of.
